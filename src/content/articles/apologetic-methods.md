# Six Ways Christians Defend the Faith

<p class="dateline">Published October 4, 2026 · Last updated October 22, 2026</p>

*Christians agree they should give a reason for their hope. Why do they disagree so sharply about what a reason can do?*

> **The question in brief.** Peter tells believers to be "prepared to make a defense to anyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you" (1 Pet 3:15). One question I've had as I've tried to live this out is: what can a reason do for a mind that is not neutral? The answers sort into six families of method: suppositional, inferential, subjectivist, relational, cultural, and prudential.

---

## What do I say to my roommate?

Hannah comes home from a weekend retreat where the speakers walked through the fine-tuning of the physical constants and the strange fit between mathematics and the physical world, and concluded that a universe this measurable and livable was built by someone who meant it to be known. The God who made her mind, they said, meant her to use it. The plenary sessions then followed Paul to the Areopagus, where he reasoned with the Greeks about the truth of the gospel (Acts 17:22–31). The later sessions drew out the rest of his strategy: he became all things to all people (1 Cor 9:22), and that takes the heart and the hands as well as the head.

Inspired, Hannah decides to share the gospel with her roommate Jess. Jess has watched Hannah pray over takeout all semester, and she listens kindly while Hannah walks through her testimony and the G.O.S.P.E.L. acrostic. Then she answers, gently and with conviction: "I don't think Christianity is true. It feels dry and divisive. And honestly, I'm not ready to give up what I've got going."

The conversation ends warmly, and Hannah is left wondering whether she said the right thing. She knows what she believes. She has no idea which objection to answer first, or what sits underneath them. So she asks around her fellowship, and by Friday she has six answers.

Marcus tells her to stop arguing, invite Jess to read the Gospel of John, and let Jess pray honestly, *"If you are there, show me."* Dana, who leads her small group, says to bring Jess on Thursday and let her see the love Christians have for one another and the hospitality they show outsiders. Ravi, an enthusiastic lay philosopher, pulls an index card from his Bible with the kalam cosmological argument penciled on it in cramped handwriting. Dr. Okafor, a retired physicist, says to get Jess to admit what she is already assuming: that reasoning works, that evidence matters, that truth beats falsehood. None of those assumptions, she says, has a home in a universe of matter in motion, so Hannah should lean on Scripture and not apologize for it. Ruth, a high school literature teacher, says to ask what is on Jess's playlists, since people confess their longings in their songs. Walt, a retired hospice technician, says to ask what Jess is betting her life on, and what happens to the bet if she is wrong.

Each answer reaches for a different one of Jess's objections. Ravi and Dr. Okafor answer *isn't true*. Dana and Ruth answer *dry and divisive*. Walt answers *isn't worth giving up*. Marcus answers all three at once by changing the question from what Jess thinks to what she has experienced.

All six advisers are on to something. Jess *ought to be* moved by Hannah's account of what God's mercy did in her own life, by Dana's hospitality, by the argument on the index card, by the plain fact that the stakes are real. But the roommate whose heart softens over a home-cooked meal with strangers may wave off the kalam, and the one who loves to argue her way toward the truth may find all that warmth suffocating. Hannah has six tools and varying skill with each. Which one God will use on Jess is not hers to know. Which one fits Jess is hers to discern, and that discernment is the work ahead.

As a physicist, my instinct is to build a unified theory of evangelism: to solve Jess as a function, vary her inputs (argument, hospitality, testimony), chart her displacement toward God, find the limit of her resistance, and estimate the critical point past which she believes. I have been glad to learn that persons are not systems of that kind. Jess is moved, if she is moved, in relationship, and each of the six methods, rightly used, is a way of knowing her better and being known by her. What can be mapped is the methods: what each assumes about final authority, about the mind that hears it, and about what persuades. I hope the map that follows helps disciple-maker and seeker alike.

## A short history of the question

The word *apologetics* comes from the courtroom. *Apologia* is a defendant's speech, and Paul uses it when he stands before the hostile crowd in Jerusalem and says, "hear the defense that I now make before you" (Acts 22:1). The early apologists, Justin Martyr chief among them in the second century, addressed their defenses to emperors, hoping to avert punishment by rebutting slanders against Christians and showing the faith to be both rational and moral. Augustine told his readers to believe in order to understand, and Anselm turned it into the church's motto for faith and reason, *credo ut intelligam*: I believe in order that I may understand. Aquinas, in the thirteenth century, spent his last years on the *Summa Theologiae*, a systematic theology longer than the whole Harry Potter series, and he built it with reason as faith's handmaiden. Some truths about God, he argued, can be proved by reason alone; others, like the Trinity, must be received by revelation.

The modern debate over method begins with the Reformation and sharpens in the twentieth century.

- **1559 — Calvin calls Scripture self-authenticating.** In the final edition of his *Institutes*, Calvin writes that Scripture is *autopistos*, "self-authenticated," and that its certainty rests on the inner testimony of the Spirit rather than on the church's approval or on proofs. He still values the outward signs of credibility, but as confirmation and not foundation (Calvin 1559, 1.7.4–5).
- **1670 — Pascal's *Pensées* appear.** Published after his death, Pascal's notes include the wager (an argument that a reasonable person, weighing infinite gain against finite loss, should stake his life on God) and the remark that the heart has reasons reason does not know.
- **1736 — Butler makes probability respectable.** In *The Analogy of Religion*, Bishop Joseph Butler argues that the Christian case need only be probable, since probability is the very guide of life, and that the objections to revealed religion bear with equal force on natural religion.
- **1846 — Kierkegaard declares that truth is subjectivity.** In the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, Søren Kierkegaard argues that historical evidence can at most yield approximation, and that faith is a passionate personal commitment no evidence can produce or replace.
- **1896 — William James turns belief into a practical question.** In "The Will to Believe," James answers the English mathematician W. K. Clifford, who had ruled in 1877 that believing on insufficient evidence is always wrong. James argues that when an option is live, unavoidable, and momentous, and the evidence cannot settle it, refusing to choose is itself a choice. In *Pragmatism* (1907) he extends the method: test an idea by the difference it makes in a life. James is Pascal's secular heir, and the progenitor of the family of prudential arguments below.
- **1955 — Van Til publishes *The Defense of the Faith*.** Cornelius Van Til argues that there is no neutral ground between believer and unbeliever, that unbelievers can reason at all only on "borrowed capital" from a Christian view of the world, and that apologetics must challenge presuppositions rather than pile up evidence.
- **1968–1970 — Schaeffer reads the culture and cites the final apologetic.** In *The God Who Is There*, Francis Schaeffer traces modern despair through art, music, and philosophy; in *The Mark of the Christian*, he argues that the love of Christians for one another is the apologetic Jesus himself gave the world.
- **1983–2000 — Plantinga argues that belief in God needs no argument to be rational.** Beginning with "Reason and Belief in God" and culminating in *Warranted Christian Belief*, Alvin Plantinga argues that belief in God can be properly basic, like belief in other minds or the past: rational without being inferred from evidence.
- **1984 — The classical school answers Van Til.** R. C. Sproul, John Gerstner, and Arthur Lindsley publish *Classical Apologetics* in response to Van Til. Romans 1 says everyone already knows God exists and suppresses it, so, they argue, believer and unbeliever share enough (the laws of logic, the reality of the world, the reliability of evidence) to build a case for God's existence before opening Scripture. Van Til's school answers that those shared tools are exactly what the unbeliever misuses, so an argument that treats them as neutral concedes the very point at issue. The same year William Lane Craig publishes the book that becomes *Reasonable Faith*.
- **1989 — Newbigin calls the congregation the argument.** In *The Gospel in a Pluralist Society*, Lesslie Newbigin, a missionary returned from India, argues that the only real interpretation of the gospel the world will receive is "a congregation of men and women who believe it and live by it" (Newbigin 1989, 227).

## Terms, with the examples built in

**1. Apologetics.** The reasoned defense and commendation of the Christian faith. It includes answering objections (why the problem of evil does not refute God) and giving positive reasons (why the resurrection is the best explanation of the evidence). A Christian who explains to a coworker why the Gospels are not late legends is doing apologetics, even if she has never heard the word.

**2. Presupposition.** A belief you reason *from* rather than *to*. Presuppositions decide in advance what will count as evidence. A person who presupposes that nothing exists beyond nature will read a report of a healing and conclude, before examining it, that the diagnosis must have been wrong. The evidence never had a chance to count.

**3. Noetic effects of sin.** The effects of sin on the mind (Greek *nous*). Scripture describes fallen human beings as darkened in understanding, not merely weak in will (Rom 1:21; 1 Cor 2:14; 2 Cor 4:4). A sports fan who cannot see his own team's foul on the replay is a small picture of the problem: the eyes work, but the heart has a stake in what they see. Apologists disagree about how deep these effects go, and that disagreement is the root of the disagreement over method.

**4. Autopistic.** From Greek *autos* ("self") and *pistos* ("trustworthy"): credible in itself. An autopistic claim cannot be validated by anything higher, because nothing is higher. Until 1960 the meter was defined by a metal bar kept near Paris, and asking whether that bar was really a meter long was pointless; it was what "a meter" meant. The analogy captures only the logic of an ultimate standard, since God is no convention like the meter, but that logic is the heart of the view: God's word is the standard by which everything else is measured, so it cannot be measured by something else (Heb 6:13–18). This article's plain name for the family is *suppositional*, because it begins by supposing God's word.

**5. Axiopistic.** From Greek *axios* ("worthy") and *pistos*: worthy of belief. An axiopistic claim is judged credible by standards shared with other claims (logic, evidence, historical method) that are external to the claim itself. A historian judging whether a letter is authentic checks the handwriting, the paper, the references to datable events; he does not take the letter's word for it. The axiopistic apologist says Christianity invites the same scrutiny and survives it. The plain name is *inferential*, because it reasons from shared premises to a conclusion.

**6. Magisterial and ministerial reason.** Reason is *magisterial* when it sits as judge over revelation, deciding what God may and may not say. It is *ministerial* when it serves revelation, clarifying and defending what God has said. A translator serves the text; an editor who rewrites it to suit himself has taken the magisterial chair. Almost every Protestant apologist claims to use reason ministerially. They disagree about what that permits, and the disagreement runs through every family below.

**7. Common ground.** The beliefs believer and unbeliever share, from which an argument can proceed. The classical apologist starts from common ground: the laws of logic, the reality of the physical world, the reliability of historical method. Van Til argued the common ground is real but borrowed. The unbeliever uses logic, science, and morality while denying the God who makes them intelligible, like a teenager arguing for his independence in his father's car.

**8. Cumulative case.** An argument in which several lines of evidence, none decisive alone, together make a conclusion highly probable. A detective rarely has a confession. He has a motive, a missing alibi, a fingerprint, and a lie about the time, and together they are enough.

**9. Natural theology.** Arguments for God's existence from nature and reason alone, without appeal to Scripture: the kalam, the fine-tuning argument, the moral argument. It is distinct from *Christian evidences*, the historical case for specific Christian claims such as the resurrection. Natural theology can reach a Creator; only the evidences reach Christ.

**10. Prevenient grace.** God's grace at work before a person responds, making a response possible. Wesleyans hold that it is given to everyone and restores the ability to answer the gospel; Reformed Christians speak of effectual calling and regeneration preceding faith. The traditions disagree about its scope and agree on the point that matters here: no argument, meal, or testimony supplies the ability to believe, and each can only be the occasion on which God does. The thaw comes before the sprouting, though the analogy stops there, since a field never resists the thaw and people can.

**11. Gethsemane epistemology.** Philosopher Paul Moser's name for the kind of knowing that Scripture calls knowing God: knowledge gained by yielding the will, as Jesus yielded in the garden ("not my will, but yours be done," Luke 22:42), and not by standing back as a spectator. Moser sets this *filial* knowledge, the knowledge a child has of a father, against the detached knowledge we have of objects. A person can learn from the manual that a climbing rope is rated for her weight and still not know whether it holds until she trusts her weight to it. The term describes what knowing God involves. It says nothing about why any particular person does not.

## What Goes Wrong

Every method below assumes something stands between the hearer and the gospel. Scripture names several obstacles, and they do not all look alike. The six methods differ partly because they aim at different ones.

The first obstacle sits in the mind. Scripture describes the fallen mind as darkened and blinded, and says so of thinking and not only of conduct: people "became futile in their thinking" (Rom 1:21), and "the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers" (2 Cor 4:4). Theologians call this the [[noetic effects of sin]], from the Greek *nous*, the mind, and the verb *noein*, to perceive or understand. The claim is not that fallen people cannot reason. The claim is that the reasoning bends. A bathroom scale that reads two pounds heavy still weighs everything, each reading works, and every one is off in the same direction. Philosophers call that systematic error, and Scripture's word for it is iniquity: crookedness, a bend that runs one way. Plantinga builds on Calvin here. In his model God made us with a sense of the divine, sin damages it, and faith is the Spirit repairing what sin damaged (Plantinga 2000 †).

The bend runs through the will as well. Paul says people "suppress the truth" (Rom 1:18), and Jesus says the light came into the world and people loved the darkness rather than the light (John 3:19). But defiance is only one entry on Scripture's list. Some stumble at the cross itself, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles (1 Cor 1:23). Some believe and will not say so, because they love the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God (John 12:42–43). Some hear the teaching and walk away from its hard sayings (John 6:60, 66). Jesus' parable of the soils adds a fourth kind: the seed that sprouts and is choked by worry and wealth (Mark 4:18–19). That obstacle is distraction, not hostility. And Scripture aims its sharpest warnings at people who say they believe: "Not everyone who says to me, 'Lord, Lord,' will enter the kingdom of heaven, but the one who does the will of my Father" (Matt 7:21), and the church in Ephesus, orthodox to the last detail, had "abandoned the love you had at first" (Rev 2:4). Belief can stop short of love, obedience, and relationship, and a method that wins agreement has not necessarily won the person.

A third obstacle is the seat reason has taken. Reason is [[magisterial|magisterial and ministerial reason]] when it sits as judge and requires God to pass an examination whose standards were written to exclude him in advance. God does not play by those rules. He hides, he calls for trust before sight, and he refuses to fit in a box built from our expectations of how a God would behave. Paul saw two versions of the mistake in the first century: "Jews demand signs and Greeks seek wisdom" (1 Cor 1:22). The signs the Jews demanded were signs on their own terms, matching a Messiah they had already drawn, and John records that "though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him" (John 12:37). The wisdom the Greeks sought was wisdom that would flatter the wise, and a crucified Savior flattered no one. The remedy is not less reason. It is reason that knows what it was made for and whom it depends on, which clarifies and defends what God has said and shown and does not rule on what he may say, do, or be.

A fourth is the cost of yielding. Paul Moser's [[Gethsemane epistemology]] takes its pattern from the garden: knowing God is completed when the will bends, as Jesus' did (Luke 22:42). Jesus puts the same point to his hearers: "If anyone's will is to do God's will, he will know whether the teaching is from God" (John 7:17). Knowing God involves handing over the controls, and a person can refuse that long after the evidence has stopped being the problem.

Nothing in the six methods removes these obstacles. Jesus says that no one can come to him unless the Father draws him (John 6:44). Luke records Lydia listening to Paul in Philippi and says "the Lord opened her heart to pay attention" (Acts 16:14). Paul supplied the argument, and the Lord supplied the hearing. That prior work is what the traditions call [[prevenient grace]] or effectual calling, and they disagree about it. Calvinists, Arminians, and Molinists give different accounts of whom God draws, whether the drawing can be resisted, and how divine sovereignty and human freedom fit together. This article takes no side, and it does not need one. All three agree on what matters for the person with a roommate to reach: God does the opening, and God does it through means.

Those means are people. "How are they to believe in him of whom they have never heard? And how are they to hear without someone preaching?" (Rom 10:14). Paul's own picture of the partnership is a garden: "I planted, Apollos watered, but God gave the growth" (1 Cor 3:6). The planting and watering are real work with real obedience attached, and the growth is God's alone. Hannah is not stepping into a room of neutral, confused people who need a clearer explanation, and she is not stepping into a graveyard where speech is pointless. Jess, like everyone, was made to know God, and according to Paul she knows enough of him to be without excuse (Rom 1:19–20) while resisting what she knows. Hannah's words are the means God ordinarily uses on a person like that, and Paul asks for prayer on exactly this point: "that God may open to us a door for the word" and "that I may make it clear" (Col 4:3–4). The door is God's to open, and the clarity is Paul's to work at.

Does better evangelism make salvation more probable? Scripture does not frame the question that way, and the traditions answer it differently. What Scripture does say is that silence is not obedience and that speaking is not a lever. Hannah can be certain of one thing: the commission to go and make disciples (Matt 28:19) binds her whether or not she can explain how it fits with God's sovereignty. A farmer who skips planting season does not get to credit God's sovereignty for the empty field.

Apply this to Jess and it forbids the one move Hannah may be tempted to make. She cannot tell which of these obstacles, if any, sits behind *I'm not ready to give up what I've got going*. It could be the cost of yielding. It could be a sober look at Christians Jess has known. It could be both, or neither. Hannah's job is not to settle which. It is to pray that the Lord open Jess's heart, to bring the method that fits what Jess actually says, and to give Jess her head, her heart, and her hands while he works. The obstacles explain why the six methods need God. They do not excuse Hannah from the commission.

## The six approaches

### 1. Suppositional: begin with God's word

The suppositional apologist denies that there is a neutral place to stand. Every reason is offered from a starting point, and the unbeliever's starting point is not Scripture's. W. Gary Phillips, whose survey chapter in *The Portable Seminary* supplies this article's two Greek method labels, calls the family *autopistic*: self-credible. Only God can vouch for himself, which is why, when he made his promise to Abraham, he swore by himself, having no one greater to swear by (Heb 6:13). The hearer is not neutral either. The fallen mind is hostile to God (Rom 8:7) and cannot receive the things of the Spirit (1 Cor 2:14), so what counts as proof to a believer and to an unbeliever may be two different things. Understanding follows regeneration, not the other way round.

Cornelius Van Til, who taught at Westminster Theological Seminary for more than forty years, gave the position its classic form (Van Til 1955). Van Til's signature move is the [[transcendental argument]]. Instead of arguing from evidence to God, it argues that God is the precondition of evidence, logic, and morality meaning anything at all. The unbeliever knows the truth, Van Til says, but not by standing on neutral ground; he stands on theistic ground he has borrowed and will not acknowledge. Theistic proofs and historical evidences keep a [[ministerial|magisterial and ministerial reason]], servant's place here, never a magisterial one: they can clarify and support what God has said, and they may not sit in judgment on it.

Phillips lists two objections to the family (†). The first is the standoff problem. The Muslim says the Qur'an authenticates itself and the Latter-day Saint says the Book of Mormon does, so if each side's evidence counts only inside its own framework, no reason can pass between them. The second is that Scripture itself seems to offer evidence to unbelievers as something they can weigh. Jesus tells his critics to believe the works, even if they will not believe him (John 10:37–38). Paul lists witnesses to the resurrection, "most of whom are still alive" (1 Cor 15:6), which is an invitation to go and ask them. The classical apologists add a third charge: to argue for God's word from God's word is circular.

John Frame's reply to the third charge is the most interesting. Every worldview's ultimate standard is circular in a broad sense, because no one can prove his final standard by appeal to something more final. The empiricist cannot prove from experience that experience is reliable, and the rationalist cannot prove by reason that reason is sound. The philosopher Mitch Stokes presses the point on skeptical unbelievers through their own patron saint, David Hume, the eighteenth-century Scot who is the skeptic's favorite skeptic. Hume showed, and Stokes agrees, that there is no non-circular argument for the reliability of the senses, of memory, or of reason itself (Stokes 2016, 41–42). Hume lived with that result by instinct; he could not help trusting his senses and his inductions, and he said so. A skeptic who invokes Hume to demand a non-circular proof of Scripture is demanding what his hero proved no one can supply for anything.

The family is right that reason is never neutral, and right that presuppositions decide in advance what evidence can do. The transcendental move is also a real weapon against any worldview, naturalism included, that cannot account for the reliability of the reasoning its own defenders use; such a worldview is in trouble on its own terms. That kind of argument clears ground. It does not by itself prove Christianity, and the careful presuppositionalist does not claim it does. What it leaves standing is a better question than "do you have evidence?" Since circularity at the foundation is the human condition and not a Christian peculiarity, the live question is which starting point can account for the reasoner who starts from it. The suppositional apologist is glad to have that question asked of Christianity, because he expects it to show the faith's superiority over its rivals.

### 2. Inferential: show that the faith is worthy of belief

Phillips calls this family *axiopistic*: worthy of belief. God made the world so that his creatures could know the truth, and John's prologue says the Word is the light that enlightens everyone (John 1:9). So the claims of Christianity are credible by the same standards we use for any claim, scientific hypotheses and historical events included, and those standards stand outside Scripture. The case is probabilistic and cumulative, and it can be put to an unregenerate mind that is still capable of weighing it.

The family has two branches. The *classical* branch runs from Aquinas through Sproul, Gerstner, and Lindsley to William Lane Craig, and works in two steps: natural theology first establishes that God exists, then the Christian evidences show that this God has revealed himself in Christ (Craig 2008). The *evidential* branch skips to the second step and divides by field. In history, Gary Habermas builds on the facts about Jesus' death and the disciples' belief that nearly all critical scholars grant, and argues that the resurrection is itself evidence for God (Habermas and Licona 2004). In philosophy, Richard Swinburne gives the family its most formal expression, a cumulative case measured in probabilities, with each line of evidence raising or lowering the likelihood of God's existence (Swinburne 2004). Phillips notes a subtlety: every evidentialist is axiopistic, but not every axiopistic apologist is an evidentialist. Aquinas is the example. He was not weighing probabilities. He held that God's existence can be demonstrated, proved from premises everyone can see to be true, and he aimed at certainty, which is a different ambition from Swinburne's and from Habermas's. One family, then, with two ambitions: proof, and the best-supported explanation.

The standard these apologists hold themselves to is more modest than their critics assume. Craig holds that a good argument need only be valid, with premises more plausible than their denials; certainty is not required. He concedes that a person might reject such an argument without being irrational (Craig 2009 †). C. Stephen Evans offers a gentler frame that sits inside the family or beside it. The classical arguments, he suggests, work best as articulations of [[natural signs]]: the wonder that anything exists, the sense of moral obligation. These point to God in a way open to everyone and easy to resist (Evans 2010). For Evans the argument's job is to make the sign legible, not to force the conclusion. That reads as an alternative to the cumulative case for readers who find the arguments too strong a claim, and as a supplement for readers who do not.

The strongest objection is that Scripture does not present the unregenerate mind as neutral. It presents it as unable to receive spiritual truth (Rom 1:18–32; 8:7; 1 Cor 1:18–2:14; Eph 2:1), and an argument addressed to a neutral judge will miss a judge who is not neutral. The common ground the arguments need is also shrinking, since people now differ about what anything means and about the most basic moral questions, such as who may live and who may die.

The reply begins with the difference between fallen and erased. The image of God remains in every person, and [[common grace]] means unbelievers see much truly: they build bridges, do mathematics, recognize injustice. Paul reasons with synagogues and philosophers alike, "explaining and proving" (Acts 17:3), and tells King Agrippa that the facts of Jesus' life were public: "this has not been done in a corner" (Acts 26:26). The inferential apologist does not claim that arguments regenerate anyone. He claims the Spirit uses them, the way Lydia's open heart came with Paul's preaching and not instead of it (Acts 16:14).

The inferential case is at its best with the honest inquirer who wants reasons and will weigh them, with the believer whose faith has been shaken by an argument he could not answer, and with anyone who has been told that Christianity requires switching off one's mind. It is weakest where the objection was never intellectual. A person who thinks Christians are unkind, or that an other-centered, cross-bearing life is unlivable, will not be moved by a syllogism.

### Between the first two: Reformed epistemology

Alvin Plantinga's work sits between the autopistic and the axiopistic, and it has reshaped analytic philosophy of religion since the early 1980s. Plantinga argues that belief in God can be [[properly basic]]: rational without being inferred from any argument, the way your belief that you had breakfast this morning is rational without evidence you could present in court. With the suppositional apologist he denies that faith must wait for arguments. With the inferential apologist he grants that belief in God can meet [[defeaters|defeater]], objections that would make it irrational if left unanswered, and that apologetics has the job of answering them (Plantinga 2000). On this view apologetics is mainly defense: removing the obstacles, not laying the foundation.

The movement takes its name from its roots in Calvin and the Dutch Reformed tradition, though one need not be Reformed to hold it. It counts as a bridge and not a seventh family because it is an account of when belief is rational, not a method for persuading anyone, and either camp can use it.

#### How I Explain Knowing vs. Showing

I distinguish, with Craig, *knowing* that Christianity is true from *showing* that it is true. The believer knows by the Spirit's witness (Rom 8:16), which is available to anyone, including a child or a farmer who has never met an argument. Argument and evidence show it to others and defend it against defeaters (Craig 2008 †). The resurrection is first a public claim, the kind of thing that could in principle have been shown false; Paul says that if it did not happen, faith is futile (1 Cor 15:17). The Spirit's inner witness is the believer's assurance of that public claim. Craig calls the witness self-authenticating, and Calvin called Scripture *autopistos* before him. I don't think either is wrong. The witness and the public claim are not rivals, since the Spirit testifies to what happened, and keep the order straight. My assurance rests on the cross and the empty tomb as events that happened, and the Spirit's witness is God confirming to me that those events are mine. A warmth that needed no cross behind it would be another thing, and not what Paul preaches.

None of this requires a believer to hold the evidence in hand. A child can trust Christ, and belief in God can be properly basic with no argument in view. Evidence can deepen a faith that came first. But lacking evidence and not caring whether it exists are different conditions. The gospel is news that Jesus died and rose, and a hearer who receives it while indifferent to whether it happened has received something other than the news. Sufficiency is not indifference.

My reasons are personal as much as philosophical. Ask me whether I will be with Christ when I die and I will say yes, without reserve, because I believe the gospel and believe my sins against God are forgiven. Scripture offers that assurance to believers so that we may know it (1 John 5:13), and it rests on Christ's finished work, not on my performance. Ask me how sure I am that God exists and I find myself reaching for a number below a hundred. I have come to think the number says more about how I was asking than about God. A probability belongs to the conclusion of a probabilistic argument, and when I put a number on God I am treating him as the conclusion of an argument. Plantinga's answer is that I need not hold belief in him that way at all; belief in God can be basic, like my belief that other people have minds. My reason and my doubts both point to God. I can still imagine there being none, the way I can imagine that I am a brain in a vat, and Hume showed that being able to imagine a thing is no reason to believe it. God has not made himself known beyond dispute. He has made himself known enough to stake a life on. When doubt presses hardest, I pray what the father in Mark 9 prayed: "I believe; help my unbelief" (Mark 9:24). Christ did not turn him away.

That order also marks the difference between Christian assurance and the "burning in the bosom" that Latter-day Saints describe as their confirmation of the Book of Mormon. I have had long, friendly conversations with Latter-day Saints, and when I have asked for evidence, the answer has usually come back to that inner confirmation. The difference I would point to is not sincerity; theirs, like that of many in other traditions, is often deep. It is that the Spirit, as the New Testament describes his work, bears witness to something that happened in public, in history.

### 3. Subjectivist: taste and see

The subjectivist family holds that Christianity's truth is validated in experiencing God's grace. "Taste and see that the LORD is good" (Ps 34:8) is its life verse. Where reason is a poor instrument for drawing people to Christ, the persuasive weight falls on encounter. In its strongest forms the family treats encounter as sufficient, sometimes at the expense of the objective truth of the biblical events. In its mildest it only sets an order: encounter first, argument after.

Søren Kierkegaard insisted that faith is a passionate inward commitment no historical evidence could produce, and Karl Barth rejected natural theology outright as humanity's attempt to reach God on its own terms (Kierkegaard 1846; Barth 1934). Martin Luther gets claimed by both sides and supplied ammunition to each. At Worms in 1521 he demanded to be convinced by Scripture and plain reason. In his last Wittenberg sermon, in January 1546, he called reason the devil's whore, meaning reason that presumes to sit in judgment on faith, since he praised it in its proper earthly role (Luther 1546 †). In popular form the family rests on testimony. The man born blind in John 9 has no argument for his critics, only an account: "though I was blind, now I see" (John 9:25).

The strongest objection is that experience can persuade without being able to compel. People who hold rival worldviews report religious experiences with equal conviction, and some claim the Holy Spirit by name. The Book of Mormon itself closes with an invitation to ask God whether it is true and to expect an answer from the Spirit (Moroni 10:4). The approach also undercuts itself when argued. If experience alone validates, one should drop every argument from experience, this one included, and simply point.

Craig answers the rival-claims objection by denying it is a defeater: another person's false assurance does not undo my true one, any more than someone else's hallucination undoes my ordinary seeing (†). The reply is sound as far as it goes, but it only protects the person who already has the witness. It gives an onlooker no way to tell the two claims apart, and the Latter-day Saint can say the same sentence back: your assurance is the false one, and mine is true. Two further points go past it. First, experiences that conflict in content cannot all be accurate, and equal intensity is not equal truth, so rival claims do not leave the question open. They put it on the table. Second, the rival claims also make public claims. The Qur'an denies that Jesus was crucified, and the Book of Mormon asserts a history of the Americas that archaeology can test. Reason can survey those claims even where it cannot adjudicate an inner glow. The worry that "every experience counts equally" collapses into relativism has substance: it makes truth whatever a sincere inner state certifies, which is relativism in devotional vocabulary.

Plantinga is the right guide here because he blocks that slide at the root. In 1982 he argued against what he called creative anti-realism, the view that our conceptual activity makes the truth (Plantinga 1982 †). He is a realist: what is true is true whether or not any person, community, or inner state certifies it, and the Aquinas he draws on holds that truth depends on God's mind and not ours. His account of warrant follows. A belief has warrant when the faculties that produce it are working properly, in the environment they were made for, and aimed at truth (Plantinga 2000). The Spirit's witness enters that account as God repairing and aiding the faculty, never as the standard that makes the proposition true. The glow answers to the world. The world does not answer to the glow.

Christian subjectivism does not have to end in theological liberalism. It only has to say what the experience is an experience *of*.

Faith is not mere assent to propositions; the demons assent and shudder (Jas 2:19). A person can know a friend loves her without being able to prove it, and her knowledge is no less rational for that. A testimony is often what makes an argument worth hearing. Experience matters. Whether it can stand alone is another question, because "taste and see" presupposes there is something there to taste, and that claim is about the world, not about the taster. This is the apologetic for the person who has heard the arguments and remains untouched, and the one least suited to the person who already suspects that religion is nothing but feeling.

Paul Moser's [[Gethsemane epistemology]] belongs here as a sub-type and not a seventh family. It names a condition that every method meets sooner or later: knowing God, as Scripture means it, is not complete at the point where the evidence is convincing. It is completed when the will yields, as Jesus' did in the garden. That puts it closest to the subjectivist family, which already treats knowing God as something the whole person undertakes, and next door to the relational family, where the yielding is learned by watching others do it. Used to diagnose an individual, it turns into an explanation of someone's unbelief that no one is entitled to give. Moser himself is careful on this point; popular uses of him are not always. Hannah can hold the account as a description of what knowing God involves. She cannot use it as a reading of Jess's heart.

### 4. Relational: the church as the argument

Jesus named the relational apologetic himself, and made it the mark by which the world would recognize his followers: "By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35). The relational family holds that the transformed lives of believers, and the love of God shown through them to believers and unbelievers alike, validate Christianity in the setting of ordinary life. Each Christian is to show love and compassion to the suffering, even to enemies (Matt 5:44; Acts 7:60), and to live in a way that earns the standing to speak (1 John 3).

Francis Schaeffer called Christian love the final apologetic, the mark Jesus gave his people to wear before the world (Schaeffer 1970). Lesslie Newbigin, after decades as a missionary in India, came home to Britain convinced that the congregation itself, the people who believe the gospel and live by it, is the only interpretation of the gospel most people will ever read (Newbigin 1989).

The family predicts a visible difference between Christians and non-Christians, and the difference is not always as clear as it should be. Hypocrisy is among the most common reasons people give for leaving the church. And many non-Christians are supremely moral people, kinder and more honest than many believers.

The Christian claim was never that Christians are better people than everyone else. It is that they are forgiven people being changed, and the change is measured against where they began, not against the kindest atheist you know. Paul says as much to the Corinthians: "such were some of you" (1 Cor 6:11). Kindness saves no one; the kindest unbeliever needs the same grace as the cruelest, and so does the believer, which is why Paul rules out boasting (Eph 2:8–9). But the reply only goes so far. If the church does not love, the relational apologetic is not merely weak. It testifies against the gospel.

Relational apologetics fits almost everyone, eventually. Arguments can remove obstacles; a meal with Christians who love each other is often what makes a person want the obstacles removed. It is weakest when it tries to stand in for truth, when "we're a loving community" becomes the whole case. When questions about whether any of it is true go unanswered, or when peace among neighbors replaces the call to repent and make peace with the God they are at war with, the strongest apologetic is emptied of its power. A Christian who hosts the best block parties, makes the best small talk, and stays silent while his acquaintances march blindly toward judgment is a pleasant, unintrusive neighbor. Paul pairs love with speaking the truth (Eph 4:15) and asks how anyone will hear without someone to tell them (Rom 10:14). Hospitality that never risks the awkward sentence has not met the obstacle of the cross, which no one steps around (1 Cor 1:23). Love that never carries the aroma Paul describes, death to those who are perishing and life to those being saved (2 Cor 2:15–16), has stopped short of love.

### 5. Cultural: why the faith seems unthinkable

Cultural apologetics holds that before a person can weigh the arguments, he has to think Christianity is the kind of thing that *could* be true. It examines why the faith seems unthinkable to so many, and then challenges those assumptions on several fronts so that the discussion can be conducted fairly.

Francis Schaeffer again, who read art, literature, drama, and music as a record of humanity's search for absolutes, purpose, and destiny, and as evidence of the bankruptcy of non-Christian answers (Schaeffer 1968). The method works on the songs I grew up with. The Goo Goo Dolls' "Iris" is a wish to be seen as one truly is when everything else is coming apart. Aerosmith's "Hole in My Soul" is the ache of somebody essential missing. Elvis Presley's "Home Is Where the Heart Is" locates home in a person and not a place. System of a Down's "Chop Suey!" reaches, at its end, for the words Jesus spoke from the cross (Luke 23:46; Matt 27:46). DMX's "Slippin'" is a man confessing that he is falling, helpless against the forces around him and the choices behind him. Audioslave's "Show Me How to Live" is a creature groaning to its maker for instructions. None of these is a Christian song. Many only voice the groaning of the human condition, and some sing idolatry into unsuspecting ears, but each shouts from the rooftops the need the gospel meets.

In recent decades Timothy Keller brought the approach to the city pulpit (Keller 2008). Charles Taylor, not an apologist but a Catholic philosopher, supplied its most important diagnosis. His *A Secular Age* asks why belief in God was almost unavoidable in the Western world of 1500 and is one option among many, often a difficult one, today (Taylor 2007). Its biblical model is Paul in Athens, who quotes the Greeks' own poets to them ("for we are indeed his offspring") before he preaches the resurrection (Acts 17:28). The modern soul is dyed the color of its playlists, and the apostle who knew to quote the Athenian poets would have known what to do with a playlist.

The strongest objection is that the analysis rests on Christian assumptions, so anyone who does not share them can reject it: the despair Schaeffer finds in modern art may be the honest acceptance of a world without God rather than a symptom of missing him. Cultural readings also age quickly. The apologetic for a culture of existentialist angst needs rewriting for a culture of distraction.

Even so, arguments are never heard in a vacuum. What a person finds plausible depends on what everyone around him assumes, and a sound argument can be dismissed without a hearing if it sounds like a relic. Cultural apologetics makes the argument audible. It also honors something true about people: made in God's image, they are creative and hungry for beauty, and that hunger points past itself. The family fits the person who has never been given a reason to think Christianity is a live option, who has not rejected the argument so much as never heard it as an argument.

### 6. Prudential: what if you are wrong?

The prudential family holds that, given the alternatives, it is wiser to believe than not to believe. The unbeliever is invited to ask what follows if his worldview is mistaken. Jesus asks it himself: "For what will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" (Matt 16:26). He also tells would-be disciples to sit down and count the cost first (Luke 14:28), which makes Jess's third objection, that the faith *isn't worth giving up* what she has, the prudential question in the mouth of a roommate.

Blaise Pascal, the mathematician who helped invent probability theory, used it to frame the wager: if God exists and you believe, you gain everything; if God does not exist and you believe, you lose little; so the reasonable bet is on God (Pascal 1670). William James supplied the modern secular version. For options that are live, unavoidable, and momentous, he argued, a refusal to decide is a decision, and the evidence need not be complete before a person acts (James 1896). James's [[pragmatism]] is also a theory of truth, that a belief is true insofar as it works in a life. Christianity cannot borrow that. Its claim is that Christ is risen. A resurrection that never happened, however much social flourishing the belief produced, would be a fairy tale with no power beyond this life, and Paul says that if our hope reaches only that far, we are of all people most to be pitied (1 Cor 15:19).

The strongest objection, like the one pressed against the inferential family, is that the argument assumes a neutral mind able to assess eternal risks, and asks a person to change worldviews on an argument that is explicitly about probabilities and payoffs. Critics add the "many gods" objection, since the same bet can be run for any religion that threatens hell, and that belief adopted as a bet looks more like fire insurance than faith. A third objection says belief is not the kind of thing a person can adopt at will.

Pascal did not think the wager could produce faith. He thought it could produce seeking. His advice to the person persuaded by the wager who still cannot believe is to stay open to belief and to begin living as believers do, and to expect belief to follow (Pascal 1670 †). The wager's real work is to show a person that neutrality is not available, because not deciding is a decision, with stakes. It fits the person who treats the question of God as an interesting puzzle with nothing riding on it, and fits badly as an opening move with someone who already suspects that Christians believe out of fear.

## How the six fit together

Phillips ends his survey on a point of agreement hidden inside the disagreement. Most apologists hold that the evidence for Christianity is objectively clear, whoever is looking at it. They divide over whether the unregenerate mind can be persuaded by it, and nearly all of them agree that only the Holy Spirit enables an unbeliever to overcome sin's effects on the mind and judge the evidence rightly (John 6:44; Rom 8:7; 1 Cor 2:14; 2 Cor 4:4). Wesleyans call the grace that goes before response *prevenient*, and Reformed Christians speak of effectual calling, and both mean that persuasion rides on a prior work of God that no method supplies.

Seen that way, the six families are not rivals at a single task. Each answers three questions differently: what is the final appeal, how deep does sin go in the hearer, and what faculty of the hearer does the method address.

| Family | Final appeal | The hearer | The instrument |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suppositional | God's word | Not neutral; reasons from borrowed capital | Challenging presuppositions |
| Inferential | Shared standards of logic and evidence | Fallen but still able to weigh | Argument and evidence |
| Subjectivist | Experience of grace | Needs encounter more than proof | Testimony and invitation |
| Relational | Lives changed by the gospel | Watching before listening | Love, hospitality, integrity |
| Cultural | What a culture finds thinkable | Formed by assumptions he did not choose | Reading and reshaping the story a culture tells |
| Prudential | The stakes of being wrong | Treats the question as optional | Forcing the decision |

The columns also map onto the obstacles above: the suppositional and inferential families work on the mind and on reason's seat, the subjectivist and relational families on the will and the cost of yielding, and the cultural and prudential families on what a person finds thinkable and what he thinks is at stake. The table is a proposal, a first attempt at a unified map. Whether it holds is open to argument, and I will return to it as the individual topics are worked through.

The methods are not mutually exclusive, and the effectiveness of any one of them may depend on the person. Someone who already sees design in the world may be ready for the cosmological argument. Someone who has just come through a painful divorce may know, better than any argument could teach him, that his worldview cannot ground love, honor, and faithfulness, and may be more open to the gospel than ever before (Phillips 2006). No one knows in advance what the Spirit will use: an argument, a crisis, a friendship, a meal.

Hannah's question about Jess still has an answer, though not a formula. Listen for which of the three sentences comes back. If Jess keeps asking *how do you know?* she is asking for the inferential or suppositional kind of help. If she keeps pointing at Christians she has known, she is asking for the relational kind. If she goes quiet when the subject turns to cost, she may be asking the prudential question without having named it. Ask her what would have to be true for the faith to be believable, and watch where she leans in. Then go further in that direction, and stay a friend while you do it.

None of the six may work, and Hannah is still commanded to try one. The listening is not a reason to wait forever. It is preparation for a move she can decide on now and make later: once she has learned enough about Jess, and earned enough trust, she makes it. The timing belongs to God, and the obedience belongs to her.

That leaves the Christian with two duties that pull against each other. The first is to know more than one method, because the method you are good at is not necessarily the one the person in front of you needs. The second is to go deep in at least one, because a method known only at the level of a slogan fails exactly when it is needed most.

## Why going deeper is worth the trouble

The simple statement of an argument is used the way a gospel outline is used. It is enough for most conversations, and you do not need a degree to use it. The trouble starts only when the simple statement becomes the whole of our understanding, when a good-faith question from an atheist gets answered with an intuition or an analogy instead of a reason.

A skeptic who asks a real intellectual question and gets back a slogan may reasonably conclude that the Christian's whole faith lives at that level: God is like a father, we are bad, we long for eternal life and perfect love, and since the unseen world behaves the way we want and the way the seen world does, we have proved everything we wanted to prove. Then he moves on. The evangelist may move on too, for reasons of his own. He wants to find as many open people as he can, and an hour with one unmoved person can look like a poor use of the time. Both stop at the surface. The evangelist should still watch for where God is moving, and get better at going beneath it.

Analogies open doors. Not everyone wants a sophisticated argument, and we have to be flexible. If the door-to-door pitch ("your current worldview is lying to you; let me switch your provider") doesn't get you inside, maybe the billboard will ("Babies prove God. Text us."), or the thirty-second video ("Christianity would look good on you"). But a mature grasp of an argument is not for chasing skeptics down every rabbit hole. It is for giving them better reasons to believe.

There is a caution on the other side, and it belongs to the believer. An argument can be persuasive without sharing Scripture's authority. The kalam, for example, is deductive in form: if its premises are true, its conclusion must be. But its premises are held because they are more plausible than their denials, not because they are beyond doubt, and the conclusion can be no more certain than the premises that carry it. When I first learned the argument, I was too certain of its truth and of its power to persuade. A Christian who leans on an argument too hard will eventually be embarrassed, for having thought that human reason, unaided by the Spirit, could move by philosophy a heart that God had not already moved by the heavens above and the moral law within. Worse, he will have taught that person to expect from reason what only grace gives.

The opposite failure is just as real. The Christian who does not care enough to give a seeker every answer he can find has mistaken indifference for humility. Peter's instruction is to be ready with a reason and to give it "with gentleness and respect" (1 Pet 3:15), and readiness means more than an analogy and a fact.

Going deeper also means meeting the objections at full strength, including the ones that shake our confidence in arguments we love. That goes for our faith as well as our arguments. It takes faith to reach someone whose ideas will test it, and going anyway is faithful. I have never met someone who did not change my life. A Christian rooted in Christ and his word can let a conversation change him without being blown away like chaff (Ps 1:4); what the wind takes was chaff, and what the vinedresser prunes bears more fruit (John 15:2). A confidence that has never met the best objection is untested, not strong. In my next article, then, I will strengthen the popular version of the kalam, and also survey its most devastating and reasonable objections: from Hume, from Kant, from philosophers of physics who deny that causation exists at the bottom of reality, and from a Christian philosopher who thinks the argument proves less than its admirers claim. The argument comes through that test better understood and more wisely held. So does the one who uses it.
